\subsection{Order-driven markets}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Outline}
% One \nocite per line -- a multi-key \nocite spanning lines breaks bibtex.
\nocite{FPR13mar}
\nocite{GPWMFH13lim}
\nocite{Bou18tra}
Order-driven markets are trading venues organised around \emph{limit orders}:
the participants supply the prices themselves,
and the venue only matches them against one another.

\pause

We describe the mechanism precisely ---
what a limit order is,
how the matching algorithm clears it,
what the limit order book is at any instant,
and what it costs to trade against it.

\pause

Two features of the data these venues produce shape everything that follows:
the events are recorded in continuous physical time,
and the occurrence of one type of event bears on the timing of the others.

\pause

Section 1.4, price formation, is the one the others are for.
It asks how a price emerges from the flow of orders,
and which statistics of that flow forecast the mid-price.
Everything in this class is the mechanism that question is asked about.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Two market infrastructures}
Market infrastructures fall in two categories:
\emph{dealer markets}, also known as quote-driven,
and \emph{limit order markets}, also known as order-driven.

\pause

Euronext, the Swiss, Helsinki, Canadian, Toronto, Australian, Hong Kong, Shenzhen and Tokyo
exchanges are purely order-driven;
NYSE, NASDAQ and the LSE are order-driven with some legacy quote-driven mechanisms.

\pause

An order-driven market is transparent:
every participant observes in real time all the others' commitments to trade,
organised in the \emph{limit order book}.
The by-product is a high-frequency record of those commitments.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{A limit order is four numbers}
A limit order is the fundamental action a participant can perform.
It is the 4-tuple
\[
(t,q,p,d),
\]
where $t$ is the time of submission, $q$ the size in shares, $p$ the price,
and $d = +1$ (buy) or $d = -1$ (sell) the direction.

\pause

A participant who \emph{posts} $(t,q,p,d)$ commits at time $t$
to buy or to sell the amount $q$ at the price $p$.
That price is the \emph{worst} she accepts:
the highest at which she buys, the lowest at which she sells.
By regulation $p$ is an integer multiple of a fixed tick size $\tickSizeOfLOB > 0$.

\pause

She may also withdraw the commitment, by \emph{cancelling} an order she has already
submitted.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{A trading epoch is the graph of a function}
A \emph{trading epoch} $\tradingEpoch$ is the collection of all limit orders submitted within
a time interval.

\pause

Orders cannot be submitted simultaneously,
so an order is identified by its timestamp.
We say this by asking that $\tradingEpoch$ be the graph of a function
from a subset of the positive half-line into
\[
\lbrace (q,p,d) : \, q \geq 0, \; p \geq 0, \; d = \pm 1 \rbrace .
\]

\pause

Timestamps are therefore unique,
which is what makes the priority relation of the next frame a \emph{total} order.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{A match trades at the resting price}
When $(t,q,p,d)$ arrives, the matching algorithm searches the active orders with timestamp
$s<t$ and \emph{opposite} direction $-d$.
An order is \emph{active} at time $u$ if it was submitted by then
and has been neither executed nor cancelled.

\pause

The active order $(s,\rho,\pi,-d)$ \emph{matches} the incoming one if $\pi d \leq p d$.
Then $\rho \wedge q$ shares transact at the \emph{resting} price $\pi$,
and the two orders are replaced by
\[
(s,\rho - \rho \wedge q, \pi, -d),
\qquad
(t,q - \rho \wedge q, p, d).
\]

\pause

At least one of the two has null size.
If the resting order is exhausted it leaves its queue.
If the incoming order is exhausted the search stops;
if it is not --- that is, if $q > \rho$ --- the search continues,
and the incoming order is matched against the next resting order in priority.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Better price first, then earlier timestamp}
The search follows the ordering
\begin{equation}
\label{eq.orderingOfLimitOrders}
 (s,\rho,\pi,-d)<(s\derivative,\rho\derivative,\pi\derivative,-d)
 \quad \text{ iff } \quad
 (d\pi<d\pi\derivative)\vee((\pi=\pi\derivative)\wedge(s<s\derivative)).
\end{equation}
Better price first --- and $d\pi < d\pi\derivative$ says that for both sides at once ---
then, at equal price, the earlier timestamp.

\pause

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma.orderingOfOrders}
 The ordering \eqref{eq.orderingOfLimitOrders} is total on each side of a trading epoch.
\end{lemma}
Antisymmetry and transitivity are those of the lexicographic order on $(d\pi,s)$.
Connexity is where the epoch enters:
two distinct orders of the same side at one price must differ in timestamp.

\pause

\begin{defi}
\label{def.orderQueue}
 $\askOrderQueue_t := \lbrace (s,q,p,-1) \in \tradingEpoch : \text{active at } t \rbrace$
 and
 $\bidOrderQueue_t := \lbrace (s,q,p,+1) \in \tradingEpoch : \text{active at } t \rbrace$.
\end{defi}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Four quantities fix the book}
The configuration at time $t$ is fixed by four things:
the best ask price $\bestAskPrice_t$, the lowest at which sell offers are active;
the best bid price $\bestBidPrice_t$, the highest at which buy offers are;
and the sizes resting at the levels of each side,
\[
\nthBestAskSize[i]_t
 = \sum_{\lbrace (s,q,\nthBestAskPrice[i]_t,-1) \in \askOrderQueue_t \, : \, s \leq t\rbrace} q,
\qquad
\nthBestAskPrice[i]_t = \bestAskPrice_t + (i-1)\tickSizeOfLOB ,
\]
and symmetrically on the bid, with $\nthBestBidPrice[i]_t = \bestBidPrice_t - (i-1)\tickSizeOfLOB$.

\pause

So the whole state is
\[
\Big( \bestAskPrice_t, \; \bestBidPrice_t, \;
\lbrace (\nthBestAskSize[i]_t, \nthBestBidSize[i]_t) : i = 1,2,\dots \rbrace \Big).
\]

\pause

In this course a limit order book always means this \emph{aggregated} book.
We do not map the sizes back to the orders of the epoch that make them up.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Size is not volume}
\begin{remark}
\label{remark.sizeVersusVolume}
 The sum of the quantities queuing at a price is the \emph{size} of that level.
 We reserve \emph{volume} for the number of shares transacted over an interval.
\end{remark}

\pause

A level has a size at every instant;
volume accumulates between two instants.

\pause

\alert{Volume is not a change in size.}
A level shrinks by cancellation as well as by execution,
so no sequence of sizes determines the volume traded.

\pause

What volume is, rather than what it is not:
by the decomposition of two frames hence,
$\volume_{t,w}$ is the sum of $\marketOrderSize$ over the market orders of the window.
It is read off the tape, and Sections 1.2 and 1.4 both turn on that.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{The spread is at least one tick}
\begin{defi}
 The \emph{spread} at time $t$ is
 $\LOBspread_t = \abs{\bestAskPrice_t - \bestBidPrice_t}$.
\end{defi}

\pause

\begin{defi}
 The \emph{mid-price} at time $t$ is
 $\midPrice_t = (\bestAskPrice_t + \bestBidPrice_t)/2$.
\end{defi}

\pause

The two sides never overlap and every price is a multiple of $\tickSizeOfLOB$,
so $\LOBspread_t \geq \tickSizeOfLOB$ whenever both sides are non-empty.

\pause

The mid-price is the quantity we shall track for the rest of the chapter.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Imbalance is bid minus ask over the total}
\begin{defi}
 The \emph{$n$-levels queue imbalance} at time $t$ is the normalised excess of the first $n$
 bid levels over the first $n$ ask levels,
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq.queueImbalance}
 \queueImbalance[n]_t =
 \frac{\sum_{i\leq n}\nthBestBidSize[i]_t - \sum_{i\leq n}\nthBestAskSize[i]_t}
      {\sum_{i\leq n}\nthBestBidSize[i]_t + \sum_{i\leq n}\nthBestAskSize[i]_t}.
 \end{equation}
\end{defi}

\pause

Bid minus ask over the total, so a bid-heavy book is positive.

\pause

The index $i$ runs over the grid, as on the picture two frames ago,
and not over the prices that happen to carry size.
On a book with a hole the two readings differ,
and Section 1.2 is where that difference is paid for.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{The micro-price weights each price by the opposite size}
\begin{defi}
 The \emph{micro-price} at time $t$ is
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq.microPrice}
  \microPrice_t = \midPrice_t + \frac{\LOBspread_t}{2}\queueImbalance[1]_t .
 \end{equation}
\end{defi}

\pause

\begin{remark}
 Equivalently
 $\microPrice_t = \big( \nthBestBidSize[1]_t\bestAskPrice_t
 + \nthBestAskSize[1]_t\bestBidPrice_t \big)
 / \big( \nthBestBidSize[1]_t + \nthBestAskSize[1]_t \big)$,
 the weighting in which each price carries the size resting on the \emph{opposite} side.
\end{remark}
So $\microPrice_t$ lies in $[\bestBidPrice_t, \bestAskPrice_t]$,
and a bid-heavy book carries it towards the ask.

\pause

The name is also given to a different object,
the martingale-adjusted limit of the mid-price conditional on the imbalance
\citep{Sto18mic}.
The two are not the same, and the literature does not always keep them apart.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Every share pays the half-spread}
Let the ask side hold at least $\quantityToLiquidate > 0$ shares at time $t$.
The quantity a buy order for $\quantityToLiquidate$ shares fills at the $i$-th ask level is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq.fillAtLevel}
 q_i := \Big( \quantityToLiquidate - \sum_{j<i} q_j \Big)_{+} \wedge \nthBestAskSize[i]_t ,
 \qquad \sum_{i \geq 1} q_i = \quantityToLiquidate .
\end{equation}

\pause

\begin{defi}
 The \emph{sweep cost} is its per-share cost against the mid,
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq.sweepCost}
  \sweepCost_t(\quantityToLiquidate)
  = \frac{1}{\quantityToLiquidate}\sum_{i\geq1} q_i
    \big(\nthBestAskPrice[i]_t - \midPrice_t\big)
  = \frac{\LOBspread_t}{2}
    + \frac{1}{\quantityToLiquidate}\sum_{i\geq1} q_i (i - 1)\tickSizeOfLOB .
 \end{equation}
\end{defi}

\pause

Every share pays the half-spread;
each additionally pays its own distance from the touch, in ticks of the \emph{grid}.
For a sell order the definition is the same with the bid side,
and $\midPrice_t - \nthBestBidPrice[i]_t$ in place of $\nthBestAskPrice[i]_t - \midPrice_t$.

\pause

Where the side holds fewer than $\quantityToLiquidate$ shares the quantity is not defined,
and code must branch on that rather than return a number.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Every limit order is a market order and a resting order}
\begin{prop}
\label{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder}
 Processing the sell limit order $(t,q,p,-1)$ against the book at $t-$ is equivalent to
 processing the ordered pair
 \[
 \big[\,(t,\marketOrderSize,0,-1)\,,\;(t,q-\marketOrderSize,p,-1)\,\big],
 \]
 \[
 \marketOrderSize := \min\Big(q, \; \textstyle\sum_{i\geq 1} \nthBestBidSize[i]_{t-}
 \indicator{\nthBestBidPrice[i]_{t-}\geq p}\Big),
 \]
 the first having priority over the second.
 For a buy the pair is the mirror image, with $\infty$ in place of $0$
 and the ask side taken under $\nthBestAskPrice[i]_{t-} \leq p$.
\end{prop}

\pause

The first component is the \emph{market order}:
the price $0$ for a sell, and $\infty$ for a buy,
is a price \emph{specification} guaranteeing immediate execution,
and not a price at which anything may rest.
The second is exactly the part that rests.

\pause

A matching engine therefore needs \emph{one} code path --- consume, then rest the remainder
--- and never a separate branch for an order that happens to be marketable.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Walking costs more than the half-spread}
A sell market order $(t,\marketOrderSize,0,-1)$ \emph{walks the book} if
$\marketOrderSize > \nthBestBidSize[1]_{t-}$:
it reaches past the best level, and each further level it reaches executes at a worse price.
A buy market order walks it if $\marketOrderSize > \nthBestAskSize[1]_{t-}$.

\pause

Walking implies $N \geq 1$ in the update rule below.
The converse fails: an order that clears the best level exactly moves the best price,
so $N = 1$, yet it prints at a single price and walks nothing.

\pause

Equivalently, wherever the sweep cost is defined, the order walks the book exactly when
$\sweepCost_t > \LOBspread_t/2$ ---
the walk term of \eqref{eq.sweepCost} being positive precisely when some $q_i$ is positive at
$i \geq 2$.

\pause

Over a window, the seller-initiated trades are the components
$(t,\marketOrderSize,0,-1)$ with $\marketOrderSize > 0$,
and the buyer-initiated trades the components
$(t,\marketOrderSize,\infty,+1)$ with $\marketOrderSize > 0$.
We adopt that as our convention for the word \emph{trade},
and keep it on venues that accept genuine market orders too.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{An order clears one level at a time}
The market part may reach any depth, and it can be decomposed one level at a time.
Writing $N$ for the number of bid levels it consumes in full,
processing $(t,q,p,-1)$ is processing the stream
\begin{equation}
\label{eq.elementaryStream}
 (t,\nthBestBidSize[1]_{t-},\nthBestBidPrice[1]_{t-},-1), \dots,
 (t,\nthBestBidSize[N]_{t-},\nthBestBidPrice[N]_{t-},-1),
 (t,q^{N}, p, -1),
\end{equation}
with priority from left to right.

\pause

Each piece is \emph{elementary}:
it either clears exactly one level, or rests without matching.

\pause

That is what the update of the next two frames is bookkeeping for,
and it is why the fold of Section 1.2 terminates: each pass empties one price.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{The update rule --- 1 of 2}
\begin{prop}
\label{prop.lobUpdate}
 Let the sell limit order $(t,q,p,-1)$ arrive at $t$, and assume
 $q < \sum_{i\geq1}\nthBestBidSize[i]_{t-}\indicator{\nthBestBidPrice[i]_{t-}\geq p}$,
 so that it does not consume the whole price-eligible side.
 Put
 \[
 N_s := \inf\Big\lbrace n \geq 0 : q < \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}
 \nthBestBidSize[i]_{t-}\Big\rbrace,
 \qquad
 N_p := \inf\lbrace n \geq 1 : \nthBestBidPrice[n]_{t-} < p \rbrace,
 \]
 \[
 N := \max(0, \, N_s \wedge N_p - 1),
 \qquad
 q^{i} := \max\Big(0, \, q - \textstyle\sum_{1 \leq k \leq i} \nthBestBidSize[k]_{t-}
 \indicator{\nthBestBidPrice[k]_{t-} \geq p}\Big).
 \]
\end{prop}

\pause

$N_s$ is where the size runs out, $N_p$ where the price constraint bites,
and $N$ is the number of bid levels consumed in full.
$q^i$ is the quantity still to be executed once the first $i$ levels are gone.

\pause

Both infima may be over an empty set.
Read $\inf\emptyset = +\infty$:
$N_s$ carries no price constraint, so it is infinite when $q$ exceeds the \emph{total} bid
size; and $N_p$ is infinite for a market order, since prices are non-negative.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{The update rule --- 2 of 2}
On the bid side,
\[
 \bestBidPrice_t = \nthBestBidPrice[1+N]_{t-},
 \qquad
 \nthBestBidSize[k]_{t} = \nthBestBidSize[k+N]_{t-} - \big( q^{k+N-1}-q^{k+N} \big),
 \quad k = 1,2,\dots
\]
and on the ask side,
\[
 \bestAskPrice_t = \bestAskPrice_{t-} - \max(0,\bestAskPrice_{t-} - p)
 \indicator{q^{\infty} > 0},
\]
\[
 \nthBestAskSize[k]_t = \nthBestAskSize[k+\delta\bestAskPrice_t/\tickSizeOfLOB]_{t-}
 + q^{\infty}\indicator{p=\bestAskPrice_t + (k-1)\tickSizeOfLOB} .
\]

\pause

Here $\delta\bestAskPrice_t = \bestAskPrice_t - \bestAskPrice_{t-} \leq 0$, and
$\nthBestAskSize[j]_{t-} = \nthBestBidSize[j]_{t-} = 0$ for $j \leq 0$.

\pause

Reading the four lines:
the bid side shifts up by the $N$ levels consumed,
and the new best bid may be partially eaten;
the ask side is untouched unless something is left over.

\pause

If $q^{\infty} > 0$ the residual rests at $p$,
improving the best ask when $p < \bestAskPrice_{t-}$
and shifting every ask index by $-\delta\bestAskPrice_t/\tickSizeOfLOB$ levels.
For a buy order the rules are the same with the sides interchanged.
\end{frame}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Volume is a sum over market orders}
Let $(\arrivalTimes[]_n, \marketOrderSize[n], d_n)$ be the stream of market orders of a
trading epoch, and $q_{n,i}$ the fills of the $n$-th of them, at prices $\pi_{n,i}$.
A market order does not in general execute at a single price.

\pause

\begin{defi}
 The \emph{volume} transacted and the \emph{value} traded over $(t-w,t]$ are
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq.transactedVolume}
  \volume_{t,w} := \sum_{n \, : \, t-w < \arrivalTimes[]_n \leq t} \marketOrderSize[n] ,
 \end{equation}
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq.tradedValue}
  \tradedValue_{t,w} := \sum_{n \, : \, t-w < \arrivalTimes[]_n \leq t}
  \sum_{i \geq 1} q_{n,i} \, \pi_{n,i} .
 \end{equation}
\end{defi}

\pause

\begin{defi}
 The \emph{volume-weighted average price} is
 $\vwap_{t,w} := \tradedValue_{t,w}/\volume_{t,w}$,
 defined whenever $\volume_{t,w} > 0$.
\end{defi}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{VWAP reads the tape, the sweep cost reads the book}
Every fill in \eqref{eq.tradedValue} prices at the \emph{resting} order's price,
never at the price the incoming order named.

\pause

\begin{remark}
 $\vwap_{t,w}$ is a statistic of the tape.
 It is a function of the transactions, and cannot be recovered from the sequence of
 configurations alone.
\end{remark}
That is Remark \ref{remark.sizeVersusVolume} again:
a level shrinks by cancellation as well as by execution.

\pause

The sweep cost, by contrast, is a function of \emph{one} configuration,
and is defined whether or not the order it prices is ever submitted.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What to take away}
The aggregated state
$\big(\bestAskPrice_t, \bestBidPrice_t,
\lbrace(\nthBestAskSize[i]_t,\nthBestBidSize[i]_t)\rbrace\big)$
is closed under the arrival of an order:
Proposition \ref{prop.lobUpdate} is written entirely in terms of it.

\pause

Every limit order is a market part followed by a resting part.
One code path, not two.

\pause

Size and volume are different words,
and no sequence of sizes determines the volume traded.
