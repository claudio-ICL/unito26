The definitions of Section \ref{sec.orderDrivenMarkets} describe a book at one instant.
This section is about where those instants come from,
and about why a participant computes them rather than receiving them.

\subsection{What an exchange disseminates}

A matching engine produces one thing: a stream of events.
It publishes that stream in two forms.

The first is the \emph{message stream}:
one message per submission, cancellation, deletion and execution,
carrying the identity of the order it concerns.
It is the tape of the orders themselves,
and the trading epoch of Definition \ref{def.orderQueue} is exactly what it transmits.

The second is the \emph{aggregated book}:
the prices and the sizes resting at each of the first $L$ levels of each side,
republished as they change.
It is the configuration
$(\bestAskPrice_t, \bestBidPrice_t, \lbrace(\nthBestAskSize[i]_t, \nthBestBidSize[i]_t)\rbrace)$
of Section \ref{sec.orderDrivenMarkets}, truncated at depth $L$.\footnote{
Both are shipped as separate products on every major venue.
Eurex publishes the message stream as the Enhanced Order Book Interface,
and the aggregated book twice over:
as the Enhanced Market Data Interface, un-netted,
and as the Market Data Interface, whose updates are netted at regular intervals
\cite{DBG24eob, DBG24emd}.
On CME's MDP 3.0 the same event appears in a market-by-order and in a market-by-price book
\cite{CMEmdp, CME26mbo, CME26mbl},
and Nasdaq publishes the message stream as TotalView-ITCH \cite{NAS23itc, NAS26tot}.
Venues and vendors call the first of the two market by order, order by order or level three,
and the second market by price, depth of book or level two
\cite{B2Bmdp, ONXmdp, EPAmdp, DBNmdp, MDLitc}.
The nomenclature is not standardised.}

The two are not independent products.
The queues $\askOrderQueue_t$ and $\bidOrderQueue_t$ are sets of orders,
and the sizes $\nthBestAskSize[i]_t$ and $\nthBestBidSize[i]_t$ are sums over them,
so the second stream is the image of the first under those sums.
The exchange computes that image by applying the matching rules of
Section \ref{sec.orderDrivenMarkets} to its own message stream,
which is to say that it runs the fold of the next subsection before publishing the result.

A sum does not record its summands:
the aggregate says how much rests at a price,
never how that size is split between orders,
nor which of them the matching algorithm reaches first.
That split is what decides whether a given resting order is executed,
and Section \ref{sec.orderDrivenMarkets} sets it aside.

\begin{remark}\label{remark.whyReconstruct}
 The aggregated book retains the quantity this chapter is about:
 how size has accumulated across the price levels of the two sides.
 That cross-section is what the queue imbalance \eqref{eq.queueImbalance} and the order flow
 imbalance of Section \ref{sec.priceFormation} are computed from,
 and Section \ref{sec.priceFormation} is the claim that the movement of the price emerges
 from its statistics.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{remark.derivedAndDelayed}
 Notice that the aggregated book is derived, and that the derivation sits in the
 publication path:
 a message is on the wire once the engine has written it,
 whereas a level update exists only once the event has been netted against the level's
 total.
 It is also truncated at depth $L$, and where the feed is netted its updates are sent at
 regular intervals, so that intermediate configurations are absent rather than delayed.
 The aggregation is left out of the message stream by design,
 price levels and their aggregation being derivable by the recipient from the individual
 orders, and the stream being published
 ``in order to send public market data as fast as possible''
 \cite[Section 3.1]{DBG24eob}.
 A participant who folds the stream herself therefore holds the aggregated book sooner,
 and to a depth the feed does not publish.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{remark.aggregatedAsCheck}
 The two feeds therefore have different jobs.
 A fold run on the message stream is the participant's own computation:
 it is available sooner and to any depth,
 and a dropped message or a mishandled event leaves it wrong
 in a way that no statistic computed from it reveals.
 The aggregated book is the exchange's own fold of the same stream,
 published later and truncated,
 and comparing the two level by level locates such an error where nothing else does.
 It is a check on the reconstruction rather than a substitute for it.
\end{remark}

A second tape exists in United States equities for a regulatory rather than a technical
reason.
Rule 603(c) of Regulation NMS obliges anyone who displays quotations,
where a trading decision can be implemented,
to display consolidated data alongside them \cite{SEC05reg}.
Consolidation is performed by a party other than the venue on which the quotation arose,
and costs a latency that a participant reading each venue's own feed does not pay.

\subsection{The book as a fold}

Writing $\mathcal{B}$ for a configuration and $m_k$ for the $k$-th message of the stream,
\begin{equation}\label{eq.bookFold}
 \mathcal{B}_{k} = F(\mathcal{B}_{k-1}, m_k),
 \qquad \mathcal{B}_0 = \varnothing ,
\end{equation}
so that the book is the accumulator of a fold and holds one state, the current one.
The sequence $(\mathcal{B}_k)_k$ is not held by the book;
it is produced by whatever drives the fold, at whatever resolution is wanted.

The transition $F$ is Proposition \ref{prop.lobUpdate}.
What makes it a single function rather than a case analysis is
Proposition \ref{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder}:
every incoming order is processed as its market part followed by its resting part,
so there is no separate branch for a marketable order,
and an implementation that writes one has two code paths where the mathematics has one.

A message is a submission, carrying an order $(t,q,p,d)$,
or a cancellation, withdrawing a size $q$ from the queue at $p$ on the side $d$;
a deletion is the cancellation of all of it.
An execution is not a third kind:
by Remark \ref{remark.passiveDirection} it is the record of the market part of somebody
else's submission, read on the passive side.
Algorithm \ref{algo.bookFold} is $F$.

\begin{algorithm}[h!]
 \caption{{The fold of the message stream into the aggregated book}}
 \label{algo.bookFold}
 \begin{algorithmic}[5]
  \REQUIRE $\mathcal{B}_{k-1}$ as two maps from price to size, and the message $m_k$
  \IF{$m_k$ is a cancellation of $q$ at $p$ on the side $d$}
  \STATE subtract $q$ from the size at $p$ on the side $d$, and remove $p$ if it reaches zero
  \ELSE
  \STATE let $(t,q,p,d)$ be the submitted order and set
         $\marketOrderSize$ as in Proposition \ref{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder}
  \STATE set $r := \marketOrderSize$
  \WHILE{$r > 0$}
  \STATE let $\pi$ be the best price-eligible price on the side $-d$
  \STATE match $r \wedge \nthBestAskSize[1]$ shares at $\pi$ if $d=+1$, and
         $r \wedge \nthBestBidSize[1]$ if $d=-1$
  \STATE subtract them at $\pi$, remove $\pi$ if it reaches zero,
         and decrease $r$ by the same amount
  \ENDWHILE
  \STATE add $q - \marketOrderSize$ to the size at $p$ on the side $d$
  \ENDIF
  \RETURN $\mathcal{B}_{k}$
 \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The loop is the reduction of Proposition \ref{prop.lobUpdate} to its elementary pieces,
and it terminates because each pass empties one price or brings $r$ to zero.
We implement it more than once,
and compare the implementations on the time and the memory they take.

\begin{remark}\label{remark.gridAgainstOccupied}
 The index $i$ of $\nthBestAskSize[i]$ counts levels of the price grid,
 whereas an aggregated feed reports the first $L$ \emph{occupied} prices of each side.
 The two agree only when the occupied prices are contiguous on the grid,
 so the queue imbalance \eqref{eq.queueImbalance} is recoverable from a truncated aggregated
 book only for those $n$ within which no gap falls.
\end{remark}

A message is an instance of \codehl{Message}:
the order $(t,q,p,d)$ as the fields \codehl{time}, \codehl{size}, \codehl{price} and
\codehl{direction}, in that order,
and a \codehl{kind}, which is \codehl{SUBMIT} or \codehl{WITHDRAW}.
Prices are integer counts of ticks, converted to currency only at the boundary,
and $d$ is the integer $\pm 1$, so that the single product $pd$ serves both sides.
A market order is a submission carrying the sentinel price of
Proposition \ref{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder}:
$0$ for a sell, and for a buy \codehl{sys.maxsize} in place of $\infty$.
A deletion is the withdrawal of the whole of one order.

The accumulator is the class \codehl{AggregateBook}.
It holds the configuration of Section \ref{sec.orderDrivenMarkets} as two dictionaries from
price to size, \codehl{bids} and \codehl{asks};
it holds neither the orders behind the sizes nor any past configuration.
Everything the book does is written against four operations on this storage:
\codehl{levels\_map}, which returns a side as a map from price to size;
\codehl{size\_at}, the size at a price, zero where nothing rests;
\codehl{set\_size}, which writes a size and removes the price when the size is zero;
and \codehl{best\_price}, which returns $\bestBidPrice$ or $\bestAskPrice$,
or \codehl{None} when the side is empty.
Listing \ref{listing.aggregateStorage} shows the last two.
The removal of a price whose queue empties is written once, in \codehl{set\_size},
and every write of the book goes through it.
The best price is a scan of the keys of a side,
\codehl{max} on the bids and \codehl{min} on the asks,
at a cost that grows with the number of prices occupied.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook.AggregateBook}: a write, and the best price. Docstrings elided.}, label=listing.aggregateStorage]
    def set_size(self, direction: int, price: int, size: int) -> None:
        # ...
        levels = self.levels_map(direction)
        if size > 0:
            levels[price] = size
        else:
            levels.pop(price, None)
    # ...
    def best_price(self, direction: int) -> int | None:
        # ...
        levels = self.levels_map(direction)
        if not levels:
            return None
        return max(levels) if direction == BUY else min(levels)
\end{lstlisting}

A message reaches the book through \codehl{apply},
which passes a submission to \codehl{submit} and a withdrawal to \codehl{withdraw}.
Listing \ref{listing.submit} is \codehl{submit}
with the recording of fills and of level changes cut,
and what remains is the submission branch of Algorithm \ref{algo.bookFold}.
Table \ref{tab.foldToCode} pairs the steps of the algorithm with the lines of the listing.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook.AggregateBook.submit}, abridged: the fills and the level changes it can record are cut.}, label=listing.submit]
    def submit(self, message: Message, record: bool) -> SubmitResult | None:
        # ...
        remaining = message.size
        direction = message.direction
        limit_price = message.price
        # ...
        last_fill_price = None
        # ...
        while remaining > 0:
            best = self.best_price(-direction)
            # ...
            if best is None or best * direction > limit_price * direction:
                break
            resting = self.size_at(-direction, best)
            traded = min(remaining, resting)
            remaining -= traded
            self.set_size(-direction, best, resting - traded)
            # ...
            last_fill_price = best
        # ...
        rest_price = self.resting_price(limit_price, last_fill_price)
        if remaining > 0 and rest_price is not None:
            resting = self.size_at(direction, rest_price) + remaining
            self.set_size(direction, rest_price, resting)
\end{lstlisting}

The code departs from the algorithm in three places.

The first is that $\marketOrderSize$ is not computed before the matching.
The loop starts from the whole size $q$,
and stops at the first best price that fails the test $\pi d \leq p d$,
which is written \codehl{best * direction > limit\_price * direction}
so that one line serves both sides.
By Proposition \ref{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder} the shares it has matched when it stops
are $\marketOrderSize$,
and the shares left in \codehl{remaining} are $q - \marketOrderSize$.
The result of \codehl{submit} reports $\marketOrderSize$ as the sum of its fills,
\codehl{SubmitResult.market\_order\_size}.

The second is the exhausted side.
The loop of Algorithm \ref{algo.bookFold} always finds a price-eligible price while $r>0$,
since $r \leq \marketOrderSize$ never exceeds the eligible size.
The code, which starts from $q$, can empty the opposite side before it stops,
and \codehl{best\_price} then returns \codehl{None}.
The test \codehl{best is None} breaks the loop exactly as a price failing the eligibility test
does, and no best price is invented for the empty side.

The third is the resting part of a market order.
Its sentinel is a price specification and not a point of the grid,
so its remainder cannot rest at it:
\codehl{resting\_price} returns the price of the last fill instead,
which is the \emph{market-to-limit} rule of the venues that accept market orders.
A market order that matched nothing has no price to inherit,
which happens only against an empty opposite side;
its shares are then reported in \codehl{SubmitResult.unfilled} and rest nowhere.

The cancellation branch of Algorithm \ref{algo.bookFold} is \codehl{withdraw}:
it subtracts $q$ at $p$ on the side $d$ through \codehl{set\_size}.
It removes at most the size resting there,
since a recorded feed begins with withdrawals of orders posted before the file did;
a book built with \codehl{strict=True} raises instead.
Each branch is exercised on a catalogue of worked transitions in
\codehl{tests/lob/test\_aggregate\_book.py}.

A session records the sequence $(\mathcal{B}_k)_k$ that the book does not hold.
Listing \ref{listing.sessionFold} is the loop of \codehl{MarketSession.from\_occupied\_levels},
abridged.
Each message is applied, with \codehl{record=False} since the fold needs no fills;
the book is then read on each side to the reported depth $L$ by \codehl{side\_statistics},
and one row is written to each of three tables.
So a session has one row per message, whatever produced the stream.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.session.MarketSession.from\_occupied\_levels}: the loop of the fold, abridged.}, label=listing.sessionFold]
        for message in messages:
            book.apply(message, record=False)
            times.append(message.time)
            index = rows.claim()
            # ...
            bid = book.side_statistics(BUY, reported_depth)
            ask = book.side_statistics(SELL, reported_depth)
            _write_occupied_levels(rows.array[index], bid, ask, price_unit, reported_depth)
            at = statistics.claim()
            touch = _write_statistics(
                book, statistics.array, at, reported_depth, spec, False, bid, ask, touch,
            )
            # ...
            traded_at = trades.claim()
            _write_trades(book, trades.array, traded_at, message.direction)
\end{lstlisting}

The three tables are the fields of a \codehl{MarketSession}.
\codehl{lobster\_book} is the aggregated book truncated at $L$,
in the column layout of the LOBSTER orderbook file:
the price and the size of each side, outward from the touch.
\codehl{stats} holds the statistics of one or two consecutive configurations:
the spread, the mid-price, the micro-price, the queue imbalance \eqref{eq.queueImbalance},
and the contribution $\orderFlowContribution$
of Definition \ref{def.orderFlowImbalance} with its window sums.
\codehl{trades} holds what each message traded, and volume-weighted average prices over windows.
The three share an index, the time of the message, named \codehl{TimeStamp};
it is non-decreasing and not unique, since several messages may carry one time.
Each table is validated against a schema when the session is assembled:
the schema of \codehl{lobster\_book} is in \codehl{unito26.lob.frames},
and those of \codehl{stats} and \codehl{trades} are declared by \codehl{SessionStatistics},
in \codehl{unito26.lob.statistics}, which fixes which statistics a session carries.

Three other drivers complete the module.
\codehl{MarketSession.from\_top\_of\_book} reads the touch alone after each message,
which is a session of reported depth $L = 1$.
\codehl{run} folds a stream through a book and records no configuration:
it returns the number of messages, fills and shares traded,
which is enough to tell that two books did the same work.
And \codehl{MarketSession.from\_delta\_log} rebuilds a session from a \codehl{DeltaLog},
in \codehl{unito26.lob.delta\_log},
which records for each message the prices whose size it changed and the new size at each.
A change stored as the new absolute size is idempotent,
so a repeated or a lost one is corrected by the next change at that price;
and since a size changes by cancellation as well as by execution,
a session rebuilt from the changes has no table of trades,
as Remark \ref{remark.sizeVersusVolume} requires.

\subsection{A faster book}
\label{sec.fasterBook}

The matching of Listing \ref{listing.submit}
is written against the four operations of the storage,
and of the four only \codehl{best\_price} does work that grows with the book.
It is called at the top of every pass of the loop,
and after the message four statistics of the session call it again:
the spread, the mid-price and the micro-price read both touches,
and the queue imbalance reads the levels counted from them.
Write $\occupiedLevels$ for the number of occupied prices on a side.
In \codehl{AggregateBook} a best price costs $O(\occupiedLevels)$,
the scan of Listing \ref{listing.aggregateStorage}.

The gain of a faster lookup is bounded.
Let $f$ be the share of the time of a fold spent finding best prices.
A lookup at no cost would leave the rest of the fold as it is,
so a faster lookup alone runs the fold at most $1/(1-f)$ times faster.
And $f$ depends on the market, through $\occupiedLevels$:
where the flow stays within a few ticks of the touch, $\occupiedLevels$ is small,
the scan is cheap, and $f$ is small with it;
where resting size spreads over many prices, the scan dominates and there is time to gain.
So the same implementation pays on one instrument and not on another.

\subsubsection{The tick is the subscript}

Prices already live on an integer grid, so a price can index an array.
\codehl{TickArrayBook}, in \codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook}, fixes a band of $\bandWidth$
consecutive prices, from a floor $\bandFloor$ to a ceiling $\bandFloor + \bandWidth - 1$.
It keeps the sizes of each side in a list of $\bandWidth$ entries,
the size at $p$ in the entry $p - \bandFloor$;
and it keeps the prices occupied on each side as one integer,
read as a set of positions in the sense of Appendix \ref{chap.integersInBinary}:
a price is occupied exactly when its bit is set.
Listing \ref{listing.tickArrayInit} is the constructor.
It keeps no map from price to size,
and deletes the two it inherits,
so that code reaching past the four operations fails rather than reading an empty book.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook.TickArrayBook.\_\_init\_\_}, with its comments elided.}, label=listing.tickArrayInit]
    def __init__(self, floor: int, width: int, strict: bool = False):
        super().__init__(strict)
        self.floor = floor
        self.width = width
        # ...
        self.ceiling = floor + width - 1
        self._sizes: dict[int, list[int]] = {
            BUY: [0] * width,
            SELL: [0] * width,
        }
        self._bits: dict[int, int] = {BUY: 0, SELL: 0}
        # ...
        del self.bids, self.asks
\end{lstlisting}

The two sides count their bits from opposite edges of the band.
A bid at $p$ is bit $p - \bandFloor$ of its integer,
and an ask at $p$ is bit $(\bandFloor + \bandWidth - 1) - p$ of its own:
each side counts from the edge of the band behind it.
The best bid is the highest occupied price, furthest from the floor,
and the best ask the lowest, furthest from the ceiling,
so on both sides the best price is the \emph{highest} set bit.
Listing \ref{listing.tickArrayWrite} shows the write and the lookup.
A write sets the size in its entry and then sets the bit, or clears it when the size is zero;
the removal of an emptied price, which \codehl{AggregateBook} performs on its dictionary,
is here the clearing of a bit.
A lookup is \codehl{bit\_length() - 1},
which by Lemma \ref{lemma.bitLengthIsDegree} is the position of the highest set bit.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook.TickArrayBook}: a write, and the best price.}, label=listing.tickArrayWrite]
    def set_size(self, direction: int, price: int, size: int) -> None:
        slot = price - self.floor
        if not 0 <= slot < self.width:
            raise ValueError(
                f"price {price} is outside the band [{self.floor}, {self.ceiling}]; "
                "a real book would shift the band or fall back to a sorted map"
            )
        self._sizes[direction][slot] = size
        bit = 1 << (price - self.floor if direction == BUY else self.ceiling - price)
        if size > 0:
            self._bits[direction] |= bit
        else:
            self._bits[direction] &= ~bit

    def best_price(self, direction: int) -> int | None:
        bits = self._bits[direction]
        if not bits:
            return None
        index = bits.bit_length() - 1
        return self.floor + index if direction == BUY else self.ceiling - index
\end{lstlisting}

Reversing the ask side makes both lookups the same operation.
Counted from the floor, the best ask would be the \emph{lowest} set bit,
which Python finds as \codehl{(bits \& -bits).bit\_length() - 1}
(Proposition \ref{prop.lowestSetBit}).
The two are not equally cheap.
An integer stores its length, so \codehl{bit\_length} costs $O(1)$;
\codehl{bits \& -bits} computes a new integer digit by digit, and costs $O(\bandWidth)$
(Section \ref{sec.binaryCosts}).
Counting the asks from the ceiling spares the ask side that scan at every lookup.

The reported levels are read by walking the bits from the touch,
in Listing \ref{listing.tickArrayWalk}:
take the highest set bit, record its price and its size, clear the bit, and repeat,
at most $L$ times for the reported depth $L$.
The walk is what \codehl{side\_statistics} calls after every message of a session,
and it visits the occupied prices only, however wide the band.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\protect\codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook.TickArrayBook.occupied\_levels}, with its docstring and its check of the argument elided.}, label=listing.tickArrayWalk]
    def occupied_levels(
        self, direction: int, reported_depth: ReportedDepth
    ) -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
        # ...
        bits = self._bits[direction]
        sizes = self._sizes[direction]
        floor, ceiling = self.floor, self.ceiling
        buying = direction == BUY
        found: list[tuple[int, int]] = []
        while bits and len(found) < reported_depth:
            index = bits.bit_length() - 1
            price = floor + index if buying else ceiling - index
            found.append((price, sizes[price - floor]))
            bits ^= 1 << index
        return found
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{A worked example}

Take a band of $\bandWidth = 12$ ticks with floor $\bandFloor = 95$, so that its ceiling is
$106$,
and a book with bids at $99$, $98$ and $96$ and asks at $101$, $102$ and $105$.
Table \ref{tab.tickArrayExample} gives the bit of each price.
The bids set the bits $4$, $3$ and $1$, counted up from the floor,
and the asks the bits $5$, $4$ and $1$, counted down from the ceiling,
so the two integers are $2^4 + 2^3 + 2^1 = 26$ and $2^5 + 2^4 + 2^1 = 50$.
On both sides the best price is the highest set bit:
bit $4$ of $26$ is the bid $95 + 4 = 99$,
and bit $5$ of $50$ the ask $106 - 5 = 101$.

\subsubsection{What is inherited, and what it costs}

\codehl{TickArrayBook} overrides the four operations of the storage and the walk,
and reads the queue imbalance and the gaps off its own storage.
The matching of Listing \ref{listing.submit}, the withdrawal, \codehl{apply} and
\codehl{side\_statistics} are inherited from \codehl{AggregateBook} unchanged,
and run on the new storage as written.
Two books that share their matching and differ in their storage
pass through the same sequence of configurations on every stream,
so they can be compared configuration by configuration,
and any difference locates an error in the storage.
\codehl{tests/lob/test\_axis\_b\_variants.py}
folds a catalogue of worked transitions and a stretch of a simulated stream through both,
and compares their configurations after every message.

The cheaper lookup is paid for on the writes.
Python's integers are immutable,
so \codehl{|=} and \codehl{\&= \textasciitilde bit} do not flip one bit in place:
each builds a new integer as wide as the band, and costs $O(\bandWidth)$,
where a write to a dictionary costs $O(1)$ on average.
A message writes a size once for each price it trades against and once for its resting part,
and it looks a best price up on every pass and for each of those four statistics,
so the trade favours the array exactly where $f$ is large.
Table \ref{tab.fasterBookCosts} collects the costs, with $L$ the reported depth;
a walk costs $O(\occupiedLevels\log L)$ in \codehl{AggregateBook},
which selects the $L$ best keys of its dictionary with a heap.

The band is fixed in advance: \codehl{TickArrayBook.for\_prices} sizes it
to the prices a stream will visit, with a margin of 64 ticks on either side.
A write outside it is refused with an error,
while a read outside it returns zero, which is true, since nothing rests there.
Its memory is $O(\bandWidth)$ whether the prices are occupied or not,
so an instrument whose prices range widely and sparsely pays for the whole range;
a production book shifts the band as the price drifts,
or falls back to a sorted map for a range too wide to index.
The sizes are a plain Python list and not an array of \codehl{numpy}:
the book reads and writes one entry at a time,
and one entry at a time is where a list is the faster of the two.

Whether the array is faster on a given stream is measured.
The stream is materialised first, so that both books fold the identical messages;
each fold is timed through \codehl{run}, which records nothing;
and $f$ is read off a profile of the fold of \codehl{AggregateBook}, as the share of the
\emph{cumulative} time of \codehl{best\_price}.
The scan calls the builtins \codehl{max} and \codehl{min},
and a profiler bills the time of a builtin to the builtin, not to the method that called it,
so the own time of \codehl{best\_price} understates $f$.
The harness is \codehl{unito26.lob.benchmark}.

Between the two, \codehl{unito26.lob.orderbook} holds three further books,
\codehl{CachedBestBook}, \codehl{HeapBook} and \codehl{BitmapBook},
each keeping the dictionaries and adding an index of the best price beside them;
the test module above checks all five against one another.

\subsection{LOBSTER}

LOBSTER \cite{HP11lob} reconstructs the limit order book of a Nasdaq-traded stock from
Nasdaq's historical TotalView-ITCH files and publishes the result at
\url{https://lobsterdata.com}.
It distributes, for each ticker and each trading day,
a \emph{message} file and an \emph{orderbook} file:
the historical, file-based form of the same pair of feeds.
The message file carries the time, the event type, an order identifier, a size, a price and a
direction;
the orderbook file carries $4L$ columns, the price and size of each side repeated outward
from the touch, padded with sentinels where a side has fewer than $L$ occupied prices.

Six event types occur:
the submission of a limit order,
its partial cancellation,
its total deletion,
the execution of a visible limit order,
the execution of a hidden one,
and a trading halt.

\begin{remark}\label{remark.passiveDirection}
 Notice that there is no event for an aggressive order.
 A trade is recorded as the execution of the \emph{resting} order it matched,
 so the direction carried by an execution names the passive side
 and the aggressor's direction is its opposite.
 In the decomposition of Proposition \ref{prop.decompositionOfLimitOrder}
 the message stream records the second component of the pair explicitly,
 and the first only through its effect on the opposite side.
 Signing a trade is therefore exact and free here,
 and inverted by anyone who reads the column as the direction of the trade.
\end{remark}

The two files are aligned by position alone:
row $k$ of the orderbook file is the configuration after the event on row $k$ of the message
file, and the orderbook file carries no timestamp and no key of its own.
Since the first file determines the second through \eqref{eq.bookFold},
the pair is the check of Remark \ref{remark.aggregatedAsCheck} in recorded form:
an implementation of the fold is compared against the exchange's own, state by state.
Section \ref{sec.lobsterEmpirics} works from these files,
and \cite{LPV22sho} is the same question asked of them at scale.

\subsubsection{A session from the LOBSTER pair}

The package reads a LOBSTER pair into a \codehl{LobsterMarketSession},
in \codehl{unito26.lob.lobster\_session}.
Its constructor \codehl{from\_files} takes the pair,
a window of the trading day and the tick grid of the instrument, and does three things.
It cuts both files to the window on the clock of the message file,
the only clock the pair carries,
and parses only the rows of the orderbook file inside it.
It checks that every price of the orderbook file lies on the tick grid.
And it derives the unit of the prices in the files from the tick size:
a unit stated wrongly does not fail, but rescales every price the session reports.
The two files are held as they are written, indexed by position,
which is the only alignment they carry.

A LOBSTER pair does not have the rows of a session.
The message file records the execution of each resting order,
so an aggressive order that consumes $k$ resting orders writes $k$ rows at one time,
with the intermediate configurations between them;
the fold of Section \ref{sec.foldImplementation} writes one row for that order,
the configuration after it.
\codehl{LobsterMarketSession.coarsened} removes the difference,
and it is the only route in the package from a pair to a \codehl{MarketSession}.
It groups the executions into \emph{market orders}:
a market order is a maximal contiguous block of executions of visible orders
sharing a time and a direction,
together with the executions of hidden orders lying strictly between two of them.
Each market order keeps its last row, the configuration after the whole order,
and the sizes and values of its visible fills are summed onto that row;
every other message keeps its row,
except a hidden execution outside any market order, which is dropped.
The aggressor of each trade is the direction opposite to the one the file carries,
by Remark \ref{remark.passiveDirection}.
The result is a \codehl{MarketSession}, with the tables of
Section \ref{sec.foldImplementation}.

The coarsening preserves two kinds of statistic exactly.
A statistic of one configuration is preserved, since each row kept is a row of the file.
So are the volume, the traded value and the volume-weighted average price over a window,
since they are sums over the fills, or a ratio of two,
and the fills of one market order share a time, so that a window holds all of them or none.
The statistics are then computed on the rows kept as on a folded session,
reading the levels by their prices on the grid and never by their columns,
as Remark \ref{remark.gridAgainstOccupied} requires;
and since a file is truncated at $L$,
a statistic that needs a price beyond the reported levels is flagged as not covered
rather than computed.
The contribution $\orderFlowContribution$ reads two consecutive configurations,
and it is preserved only in part.

\begin{remark}\label{remark.coarseContribution}
 Let the fills of one market order be the rows $j+1, \dots, j+K$ of the file,
 so that the coarse session keeps the row $j+K$ and its contribution is that
 of the transition from the configuration of row $j$ to that of row $j+K$.
 This equals $\sum_{n=j+1}^{j+K} \orderFlowContribution$
 when every visible fill is at the best price facing the order on arrival.
 When the order walks the book, the row kept registers the size at that best price alone,
 which is what a fold of the order registers.
\end{remark}

The package reads the configurations the exchange computed, from the orderbook file,
and does not fold the message file again.
Folding it, and reconciling the result with the orderbook file,
is part \emph{i.} of Assignment \ref{assignment.orderFlowImbalance}.
